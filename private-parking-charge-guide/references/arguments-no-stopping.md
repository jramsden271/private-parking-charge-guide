# Argument library — no-stopping and airport charges

Part of the private-parking-charge-guide argument library (groups NS and AP). **Read `arguments.md` first**: its contents list, "How to use" rules, "Gathering evidence" section and IAS note all apply here, and the arguments here often combine with its core groups (L, C, CP, M, CT). Argument IDs are stable across files.

## NS — No-stopping cases

These apply where the sign is a prohibition ("No stopping", "No stopping, picking up or dropping off") rather than terms for parking. They're common at airport and station forecourts and on private access roads. Check L3 (relevant land) first, because many of these sites are byelaw land. **But railway stations became relevant land on 26 Dec 2025.** For a station incident on or after that date, L3 is gone; rely on L1 (NTK compliance), NS10 and L4 for the keeper, and the contract and signage points (NS1–NS3, NS6–NS8) otherwise. Airports are unaffected. For airports, also read the AP section for the forum's airport playbook (thread 6500170).

**The Code's own standard for these zones (Single Code v1.1).** It describes no-stopping zones as "private roads clearly marked with lines and clear, obvious, and repeated traffic-facing 'no stopping' signs and barriers to deter trespass" (Annex F.2.1). Annex B gives them a consideration period of "while driving" and no grace period. F.2.1 also lists stops that must be cancelled (see NS6).

**Key authority: *VCS v Ozog* [2026] EWCC 12** (HHJ D. Walsh, 30 March 2026; Leeds Bradford Airport roadways; first appeal on no-stopping). This was VCS's appeal against a District Judge's finding that no contract was formed. The outcome was mixed, and VCS lost both claims:
- **For operators:** the appeal judge rejected any general rule that no-stopping signs can't form a contract. "Each case turns on its facts". The judge found that the signs **implicitly** offered permission to enter the land, with "no stopping" as a condition. The signs didn't expressly offer permission to enter; the offer was read into them. Keep that distinction when citing *Ozog*: an implied offer is easier to dispute where the driver already had a right to be there (NS1). Where the driver could have stayed on the public road (Whitehouse Lane) and not entered, entering past the signs was acceptance by conduct. See NS1 (whether the sign offered anything new) and NS8 (whether there was a real choice).
- **For motorists:** "no stopping" doesn't mean no stopping for any reason. A stop forced by circumstances (here, a malfunctioning barrier) is "a matter of compulsion, and thus not within the 'no stopping' term" (NS6).
- **For motorists:** the operator must prove the vehicle actually passed the signs. The second claim failed for lack of that evidence (NS7).

The judgment is published (BAILII / National Archives; also in thread 6672354). Cite it accurately: it's a County Court appeal, so persuasive rather than binding.

### NS1. A prohibition isn't a contractual offer, unless it comes bundled with something new
- **Point:** a sign saying "No stopping" forbids an act. On its own it offers nothing in return, so there's no consideration and no contract. *Ozog* doesn't overturn this. As we read it, the appeal judge found a contract because the signs offered something: permission to enter and use the private land, with "no stopping" as a condition attached to that licence. That offer was **implied**. The signs didn't expressly offer permission to enter; the judge read it into them. The no-stopping term was enforceable only because it came bundled with that consideration. **So the question is whether the driver already had the right to be there.** If permission to enter or pass already existed, there's nothing for the sign to offer, so no offer of permission can be implied, and the sign reverts to being a bare prohibition. Point out that the signs themselves say nothing about granting permission. Examples:
  - an implied or public right of access;
  - a right to pass under byelaws or road traffic legislation;
  - a separate contract already made, e.g. pre-booked airport parking (but a car-park receipt can reveal the route the car took, so for NS7 cases hold it back until the witness statement: rule 11);
  - a residential right of way.
- **Applies when:** the sign's main message is a prohibition, the charge is presented as contractual, **and** the driver already had a right to be on the road (see the examples), or the signs don't actually offer any licence to enter.
- **Evidence:** the sign wording (does it offer anything, or only forbid?); proof of the existing right (booking confirmation, byelaws, public access, lease).
- **Strength:** Moderate where an existing right can be shown. Weak where the only right to enter came from the signs themselves (*Ozog*). Be candid about this. *Confidence: Medium* (this reading of *Ozog* should be checked against the judgment text before it's relied on in court).
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal:* brief.
  - *POPLA and IAS:* set out offer, acceptance and consideration, and the existing right.
  - *Defence:* deny that any contract was formed; plead the existing right and that no fresh consideration was offered.
  - *WS:* expand, with the booking, byelaws or other proof of the right, and the sign photos.
- **Established wording:**
  - *(defence)* "A sign prominently stating 'No Stopping' is an absolute prohibition. It does not offer a contractual service or licence; it forbids an action. A forbidden act cannot form the basis of an offer, and no consideration or benefit is offered to the motorist in return."
  - *(defence, existing contract; from a forum defence)* "The only 'parking contractual terms' that the Defendant agreed to at the Airport were those relating to the pre-paid parking, which was concluded online in advance."
- **Forum threads:**
  - 6637588.
  - 6672354 (*Ozog*). Bazarius argues an operator can't offer a "contractual licence to pass" when a right to pass already exists under byelaws or road traffic legislation.
  - 6430731 (Leeds Bradford Airport defence: pre-paid parking contract; byelaws).

### NS2. "Accepted by parking" vs a "no stopping" breach
- **Point:** if the Particulars say the contract was accepted by parking, and the breach was stopping, two things follow:
  - a vehicle that only stopped never parked, so on the claimant's own wording there was no acceptance;
  - if stopping is treated as parking, the act needed to accept the contract is itself the breach, which makes the terms impossible to perform.
- **Applies when:** the Particulars or terms say acceptance is "by parking" (or similar), and the alleged breach is "no stopping".
- **Evidence:** the exact words of the Particulars; ANPR or CCTV times showing how long the vehicle was stationary.
- **Strength:** Strong when pleaded that way.
- **By stage:**
  - *Defence:* the lead point.
  - *WS:* expand it.
  - *POPLA and IAS:* usable, quoting the sign or NTK wording instead.
- **Established wording:** *(defence)*
  - "The POC state that a contractual licence was accepted 'by parking'. Stopping is not parking (*Homeguard Services Ltd v Jopson* [2016]). The vehicle was stationary for a mere [duration]. If the driver did not park, then by the Claimant's own pleaded wording, no contract was accepted."
  - "In the alternative, if the Claimant asserts that stopping constitutes parking, the terms are void for impossibility. The POC state the contract is accepted 'by parking' yet assert the breach was 'No stopping'. A vehicle cannot park without first stopping. The Claimant argues that the very act required to accept the contract is simultaneously an immediate breach of it."
- **Forum threads:** 6637588.

### NS3. Not a properly marked no-stopping zone: no red lines or repeater signs
- **Point:** road markings aren't contractual terms in themselves. But a no-stopping zone must be obvious to a moving driver, because there's no time to stop and read (the Code's consideration period is "while driving"). That means clear lines (red lines or hatching), a large entrance sign, and repeated traffic-facing "no stopping" signs throughout. Without these, the operator can't show where the prohibition applied, or that a driver could have known. Their absence is a strong point for the defendant. The Code's own description of these zones says the same. POPLA's former Lead Adjudicator, barrister Henry Greenslade, said in a POPLA annual report that ordinary wordy signs are unsuitable, and described what a no-stopping zone must look like: red lines and repeater signs.
- **Applies when:** there are no red lines (or only yellow lines, or faded markings); no repeater signs facing oncoming traffic; only small, wordy signs; the road is laid out like a public road; the zone's boundary is unclear; the terms are in small print or behind online links.
- **Evidence:** photos and video from a driver's position along the route, showing the absence of lines and repeater signs; a site plan; the operator's own photos.
- **Strength:** Strong where the lines and repeater signs are missing. Weak at sites with red lines and repeated signs (e.g. many airport forecourts).
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal:* one line ("not a properly signed or marked no-stopping zone").
  - *POPLA:* quote the Code (Annex F.2.1) and refer to Henry Greenslade's POPLA annual report; attach photos.
  - *IAS:* as POPLA. IAS assessors will be familiar with the POPLA annual report.
  - *Defence:* plead that the zone isn't properly signed or marked, citing the Code's description.
  - *WS:* photos as exhibits, plus the Code extract and the POPLA annual report extract if obtainable.
- **Established wording:**
  - *(POPLA/IAS, forum-tested)* "'No stopping' markings should be red and there is an annual report from POPLA … where Lead Adjudicator, barrister Henry Greenslade refers to what a no stopping zone must look like with red lines and repeater signs. These signs do not exist at the entrance to [site] nor are there repeater signs through the area."
  - *(defence)* "The private parking sector Single Code of Practice describes no stopping zones as private roads clearly marked with lines and clear, obvious and repeated traffic-facing 'no stopping' signs and barriers to deter trespass. The Defendant argues that this is not a properly or fairly signed no stopping zone. The access road is laid out to appear like a public road [and includes double yellow lines next to kerbs]."
  - *(defence, signage)* "The Claimant's signage fails to satisfy the legal requirements of prominence and clarity. The signage fails to clearly demarcate the boundary of any restricted zone. … material terms (including the £[x] fee) are displayed in minuscule font or obscured via online hyperlinks, which no driver could comprehend whilst driving."
- **Forum threads:** 6536465 (Bilbrough: yellow lines aren't a no-stopping restriction; text too small to read on the move); 6071461 (Gateway House: signs refer to "double yellow lines"); 6639631 (POPLA/IAS wording, PPS Western Gateway); 6613939 (Coupon-mad: search "Henry Greenslade repeater signs red route" and quote him); 6565865 (UKCPS/Moorside defence wording, 2025); 5280358 (older POPLA appeal citing the Lead Adjudicator); 6637588.

### NS4. Trespass is the landowner's claim, not the operator's (a passing remark only)
- If no contract was formed (NS1), stopping is at most a trespass, and only the landowner could sue for that; it hasn't. Use as a one-sentence aside in a defence or WS, never as a standalone point.

### NS5. No legitimate interest in penalising a momentary stop
- **Point:** *Beavis* allowed a charge where the operator had a legitimate interest in managing the car park. Penalising a stop of a few seconds, with no obstruction and at a quiet time, serves no such interest, so the charge is a penalty.
- **Applies when:** the stop was very short; there was no obstruction or safety issue; it was at a quiet time.
- **Evidence:** the operator's own times or photos; the time of day.
- **Strength:** Supporting.
- **By stage:**
  - *Defence:* brief.
  - *WS:* expand it.
- **Established wording:** *(defence)* "The vehicle was observed stationary for [duration] at [time], a time when traffic is minimal. The Claimant has no 'legitimate interest' (*ParkingEye v Beavis* [2015]) in forbidding or penalising such an inconsequential event, as it serves no genuine traffic management or commercial purpose."
- **Forum threads:** 6637588.

### NS6. A stop that wasn't a choice isn't a breach
- **Point:** "no stopping" doesn't mean no stopping for any reason whatsoever. A stop forced by circumstances isn't within the term (*Ozog*: a malfunctioning barrier; the judgment's examples of other limits include a child choking, an engine fire, or a protester in the road). The judgment contrasts these with voluntary stops, such as checking football scores on a phone or looking for a mint in the glovebox. The Code agrees: F.2.1 says charges in no-stopping zones should be cancelled where the stop was "to avoid an accident"; "to allow a person to get or give help in respect of an accident or emergency"; "to give way to an emergency vehicle"; "at a closed gate or barrier"; for pedestrians on a zebra crossing; or "due to stationary or slow-queuing traffic preventing the vehicle from moving". It also covers F.1(a)–(d) (emergency vehicles, doctors on call, breakdown recovery, vehicles directed in).
- **Applies when:** the evidence shows the stop was forced: queuing traffic, a barrier or gate, an obstruction, an emergency, a passenger taken ill, or an instruction from staff or police. Being lost or disorientated by a confusing layout is weaker but has been argued (6565865).
- **Not covered:** stopping to let a passenger in or out at a gate or crossing (the Code expressly excludes this), or any stop that was a choice.
- **Evidence:** the operator's CCTV/photos (often showing traffic or the barrier); dashcam footage (F.2.3 asks for footage if available); witness accounts.
- **Strength:** Strong with evidence (*Ozog*; Code F.2.1).
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal:* lead with it, and cite F.2.1.
  - *POPLA and IAS:* the operator's failure to cancel under F.2.1 is a Code breach (CP3).
  - *Defence:* even if a contract existed, there was no breach, because the stop was compelled (*Ozog*).
  - *WS:* the facts and evidence.
- **Established wording:** *(from Ozog)* "The stoppage cannot be described as a matter of choice, and can only be regarded as a matter of compulsion, and thus not within the 'no stopping' term."
- **VCS's own concession:** in *Ozog*, VCS's counsel accepted that "no stopping" has limits. VCS has elsewhere said the reason for stopping is legally irrelevant. Point out the inconsistency if the operator takes the absolute line (6672354, p2).
- **Also:** a complaint to the landowner (e.g. the airport) has worked for no-stopping charges even where the operator refused (6611550, 6558881).
- **Forum threads:** 6672354; 6611550; 6558881; 6565865.

### NS7. The operator must prove the vehicle passed a sign on the route it actually took
- **Point:** acceptance by conduct needs the driver to have passed the signs. A photo of the car stationary on the road doesn't prove it entered past a sign, or by which route. At airport, station and private road networks there's often more than one way in: the main entrance, internal links, **car park exits that feed straight into the no-stopping road**, service roads. If there was no sign between where the car actually joined the road and where it stopped, no terms were offered on that route, so no contract was formed, whatever the main entrance shows. In *Ozog*, the second claim failed because VCS couldn't show the vehicle had passed the signage. In *VCS v Jones* (Bristol Airport, 2025), VCS lost because it couldn't prove where its photos were taken in relation to the signs.
- **Applies when:** the site has several entrances or internal routes; the car joined from a car park, drop-off area or side road rather than the main entrance; the operator's evidence is a still with no route shown; the stop was at or near the boundary.
- **Evidence:** a site plan or map showing every way in and every sign (the user's own photos of the route taken, or dated Street View: see "Gathering evidence" in `arguments.md`); the operator's photos and any map or plan it has (or its absence).
- **Strength:** Strong where the route taken had no sign, or the operator can't show where the car was relative to its signs; Moderate otherwise.
- **Tactics: don't show the operator the gap too early** (see rule 11, "Don't show your whole hand early", in `arguments.md`). *Jones* was won on a missing map. Had the defence spelled out the route point, VCS might simply have added a site plan to its witness statement. So:
  - *Appeal, POPLA and IAS:* usually leave it out, or put the operator to proof of its case in general terms. Raise it specifically only if it's likely to win at that stage on its own (e.g. a POPLA comment once the operator's evidence pack plainly has no route or sign map).
  - *Defence:* keep it general and short: deny that any contract was formed or breached, and put the claimant to strict proof of the contract, the terms relied on, the signage on the vehicle's route, and where the vehicle was. If the user was the driver and the Particulars name them as driver, the defence must still give their own version where it differs (CPR 16.5), so a bare factual line such as "The vehicle entered the road from [X], not from [the main entrance]" may be needed, without explaining its significance. Check the wording with the forum. A **keeper who wasn't the driver** has no first-hand version to give, so the general strict-proof wording is enough; second-hand route details can wait for the witness statement (via the driver's evidence, if they choose to give it).
  - *WS and hearing:* make the point in full: the route taken, photos or a plan showing no sign on it, and the gaps in the claimant's evidence. Small-claims witness statements are usually exchanged at the same time, so the claimant has little chance to fill the gap before the hearing.
  - *Risk:* a judge can allow late evidence or adjourn, and a point that appears for the first time at a hearing can be criticised. Holding back is a common forum tactic, not a guarantee. *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Defeated by / careful with:** a driver who has said (or will say in a statement of truth) that they came in past the signs. The defence can still put the claimant to proof of its own evidence (where its photos were taken, which signs, CCTV), but must never suggest the car took a route it didn't. Stops at a car-park entry barrier are better argued under NS6 (compelled stop).
- **Established wording:** *(none yet)*
- **Also:** check where the operator's photos were taken, ask for its CCTV, and note any refusal. In *Jones*, VCS's witness statement came from someone who hadn't seen the incident, and the judge noted that the court, not the claimant, decides whether there was a breach.
- **Forum threads:** 6672354; 6625532 (*VCS v Jones*).

### NS8. No opportunity to show alternative conduct, so no acceptance by conduct
- **Point:** acceptance by conduct only works if the driver had a real choice. They must have been able to read and understand the terms in time, and then to behave differently to show they *weren't* accepting them (driving on, turning back). If the layout commits the driver before the terms can be read, entering isn't acceptance. It's simply the only thing they could do. This is the basis of the District Judge's reasoning in *VCS v Ward*: the first sign was at a roundabout exit, so the driver "had no realistic opportunity to come to a halt and take a reasoned decision". *Ozog* distinguished *Ward* on its facts: Mr Ozog could have stayed on Whitehouse Lane. The question is therefore always fact-specific. A sign is no use if the driver has already had to choose a lane or exit before it could be read, or if the terms can't be read and understood in the seconds available at driving speed.
- **Applies when:**
  - the first sign is at or just after a roundabout exit or junction;
  - lane markings commit the driver before the sign is visible;
  - there's no turning point or route back, so turning around is itself prohibited (e.g. red lines with "no stopping or turning");
  - the terms are too long or too small to read at speed.
- **Evidence:** a site plan or map showing the approach, lanes and first sign; dashcam footage; photos from the driver's position at the decision point; a rough calculation of the reading time available at the speed limit. This argument rarely works without evidence of the layout. If the user has none, ask them to revisit the site, or use dated Google Street View screenshots of the approach (see "Gathering evidence" in `arguments.md`).
- **Strength:** Strong where the layout really gives no choice (*Ward*); Weak where there's a clear, early choice point (*Ozog*). Fact-specific, so recommend the forum.
- **Related:** Consumer Rights Act 2015 Sch 2 (a term binding a consumer "with which the consumer has had no real opportunity of becoming acquainted before the conclusion of the contract"); *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* [1970] EWCA Civ 2 (terms must be brought to the customer's attention before the point of contract); CT6.
- **By stage:**
  - *POPLA and IAS:* describe the layout, with a map and photos.
  - *Defence:* plead that there was no realistic opportunity to read the terms or to decline them, so no acceptance by conduct.
  - *WS:* site plan, photos and timings as exhibits.
- **Established wording:** *(none yet; check the forum for tested phrasing)*
- **Forum threads:**
  - 6672354. yarpsnehpets: airport layouts don't give the motorist an opportunity to demonstrate alternative conduct; drivers must be in the correct lane well before they first see the signs.
  - 6565865 (Gateway House: satnav directions to the station lead drivers onto the private road; an "Entrance" sign makes it look like a public route into a car park).
  - 6669616 (Warburton Bridge). On byelaws rather than contract, but the same fairness point: once on the approach road, drivers are committed, with double red lines and no stopping or turning.

### NS9. The site wasn't properly approved for no-stopping charges
> **Keep this point in proportion.** It's a supporting Code point, never the lead and never the whole case. Use it only after the stronger NS, L and C points, in a sentence or two. Don't let it take over an appeal or defence.

- **Point:** the Code treats stopping charges as exceptional. Single Code v1.1, cl. 5.3 "Prohibition on stopping" (BPA and IPC members alike): the Code "applies to instances where the prohibition on stopping arises from a clear security concern e.g. within airports. Parking operators must only pursue parking charges in instances that could be interpreted as stopping if they have explicit consent to do so on evidenced security or safety grounds from their ATA, following audit of the adequacy of the signs and surface markings in place to inform drivers of the terms and conditions in place." The trade-body oversight section repeats the audit requirement. So put the operator to proof that it had its trade body's explicit consent, on evidenced security or safety grounds, after an audit of the signs and markings, before charging at this site. Also check whether the site matches the Code's own description of a no-stopping zone (NS3).
- **Which Code applies (check the incident date):**
  - **Incident on or after 1 Oct 2024, any operator (BPA or IPC):** Single Code cl. 5.3. Caveat: the Code's transition table gives existing sites until **31 Dec 2026** to comply with "signage or other related clauses". An operator may argue cl. 5.3 is site-related and didn't yet bind an older site. That's untested, so frame the point as "put to proof", not as a certain breach.
  - **Incident before 1 Oct 2024, IPC operator:** the old IPC Code v9, s.13.3 had similar wording (as quoted on the forum; check the text before relying on it).
  - **Incident before 1 Oct 2024, BPA operator:** the old BPA Code applied. Its no-stopping wording hasn't been checked, so don't use this point without the text.
- **Applies when:** a no-stopping charge at a site with no obvious security or safety reason (an office access road, a retail park, a station approach such as Gateway House). It's weaker at airports, where the Code itself gives security as the example.
- **Strength:** Supporting only. It's a Code point, so it doesn't bind the court, but it helps at POPLA and IAS and shows poor practice.
- **By stage:**
  - *POPLA and IAS:* ask for evidence of the trade body's consent and audit.
  - *Defence:* one brief supporting paragraph at most.
- **Also:** cl. 5.3's opening words support the keeper point in NS10.
- **Established wording:** *(none yet)*
- **Forum threads:** 6536465.
- **Sources:** Single Code v1.1 cl. 5.3, the transition table, and the trade-body oversight section (`notices-and-codes.md`); IPC Code v9 s.13.3 (old; check the text).

### NS10. A momentary stop isn't "parking", so POFA keeper liability can't attach
- **Point:** POFA Sch 4 lets an operator recover unpaid "parking charges" from the keeper. It's framed around a vehicle that has been *parked*. A car that stopped briefly while being driven, with the driver at the wheel, wasn't parked, so there was no parking event and keeper liability under POFA can't arise. The operators' own Code accepts the awkward fit: cl. 5.3 says POFA's provisions "relate specifically to the parking of vehicles" and envisage "a stationary, generally unoccupied, vehicle".
- **How to use it:** not on its own. It's one strand of the **no-keeper-liability package** for a keeper who hasn't named the driver: (1) the land isn't relevant land (L3) or the NTK fails POFA (L1); (2) **and in any case a momentary stop isn't parking (NS10)**; (3) so there's no keeper liability and no presumption that the keeper drove (L4). It also supports C4 (stopping isn't parking) on the contract side.
- **Applies when:** a keeper (driver not identified) receives a no-stopping or drop-off charge where the car was stationary only briefly with the driver in it.
- **Defeated by:** the driver being identified (then POFA isn't needed); a long stop with the car left unattended (that looks like parking).
- **Strength:** Supporting. Not tested in a reported case that we know of; the forum regulars use it as part of the keeper argument at Gateway House ("no parking event, so no keeper liability"). *Confidence: Medium.*
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal and IAS:* one sentence inside the keeper-liability paragraph.
  - *POPLA:* the same, quoting cl. 5.3.
  - *Defence:* plead it alongside L1/L3/L4.
- **Established wording:** *(none yet; suggested keeper-safe phrasing)* "The vehicle was, at most, momentarily stationary on a road; it was not parked. Schedule 4 of the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 applies only to parking charges for a parked vehicle, so keeper liability cannot arise." Don't say who was in the car or why it stopped.
- **Forum threads:** 6071461; 6565865.
- **Sources:** POFA 2012 Sch 4 (`notices-and-codes.md`); Single Code v1.1 cl. 5.3.

## AP — Airport drop-off charges

Main forum source: Coupon-mad's group thread **6500170** ("Heathrow Gatwick Manchester & other Airport drop-off PCNs - easy: how to appeal & win"; opened Jan 2024, edited Aug 2025, still active). Its headline advice is not to pay drop-off PCNs. Check the latest posts before advising, since the thread says the advice will change when the government's statutory regime arrives (don't predict that regime; see SKILL.md section 9).

**For a keeper, at any airport, lead with no keeper liability** (L3 byelaw land, NS10, L4), whichever kind of charge it is; then add the kind-specific points. Check the airport's byelaws cover stopping or parking (6654021).

**Next, work out which kind of airport charge it is.**
| Kind | Typical operators and airports (per 6500170) | Trade body | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paid drop-off zone**: the fee wasn't paid by the deadline | APCOA (Heathrow, Manchester, Luton, Birmingham); NCP (Gatwick); NSL (Stansted) | BPA, so POPLA | AP1, AP2, L3, L4 |
| **No-stopping roadways** | VCS (Bristol, East Midlands, Leeds Bradford, Southend); UKPPO (Newcastle) | IPC, so IAS | The NS group, plus L3 and L4 |
| **Airport car parks** (short-stay, multi-storey, long-stay, pre-booked): overstay, unpaid or underpaid tariff | Varies by airport; check the notice | Check the notice | AP1 if the car park is inside the byelaw boundary, plus L4; then the ordinary points (CP1, C6, C1, L5) as secondary. A delayed flight is mitigation (M1), better saved for a landowner complaint than an appeal. A pre-booked parking contract has its own terms: check them |

Airport operators change, so check the NTK rather than assuming.

**The forum's playbook (6500170):**
- **Appeal time still open:** a keeper appeal, saying airport byelaw land isn't relevant land, so there's no keeper liability (AP1). Don't name the driver, and don't tick any online box that identifies who was driving. APCOA and NCP usually cancel at this stage, according to the thread.
- **Appeal time expired (APCOA/NCP):** send the same wording as a **complaint**, not an appeal. APCOA usually backs off. NCP may pass it to BW Legal, whose threats or claims are described as easily defeated while the driver stays unidentified.
- **POPLA:** if the operator issues a POPLA code instead of cancelling, appeal on AP1 and AP2. The thread expects the operator to withdraw.
- **NSL (Stansted):** described as worse, with NTKs said to imply POFA keeper liability that can't apply (AP2). Regulars have complained to the BPA and DVLA about this.
- **VCS / UKPPO (IPC):** one initial appeal as keeper; IAS is usually not worth escalating to (see the IAS note). Debt-collector letters can be ignored. A genuine Letter of Claim needs a decision (replying is optional; SKILL.md stage D), and any claim must be defended. A VCS claim is described as defendable; use the NS group, L3 and L4. **The user must keep their address up to date with DVLA and the operator**, so a claim can't go to an old address and become a default CCJ.
- **Any claim:** follow the normal court process (`court-claim-and-defence.md`).
- **Several charges** (e.g. a taxi driver or regular commuter): treat each separately but the same way, and appeal each on time; keep a log of dates and references. If they reach court, they may be bundled into one claim. Get forum help early if there are many.
- **A complaint to the airport** (the landowner) can run alongside the appeal. It has worked at Leeds Bradford for no-stopping charges (6611550, 6558881). Keep it about the operator's conduct and the signs, not who was driving.
- **Apologies and goodwill offers.** Paying is always the user's choice (SKILL.md section 1). But don't mix an apology or an offer of money into a keeper appeal: it reads as an admission and undermines the no-keeper-liability point. If the user wants to pay the drop-off fee (or has already paid it late), that can be done separately; mention a payment in the appeal only as a fact, and only if it doesn't identify the driver.
- **Whatever the story, lead with no keeper liability.** Paid late, paid with the wrong registration, didn't know there was a fee, several charges from repeat drop-offs: the forum's view is that the byelaw keeper appeal (AP1, with NS10 and L4) is still the most reliable route to cancellation at airports. Don't let the facts tempt the user into explaining who was driving or apologising for not paying. Add a fact-specific point (e.g. proof of a late or mistyped payment, CP2; unclear fee signs, C1) only as a short secondary line, and never in a way that identifies the driver.

**Cautions:**
- **Check the byelaw boundary and the byelaws themselves** for that airport (6654021). VCS has replied that some airports' byelaws are obsolete, and an MP has disputed the byelaw argument (6500170). Don't state the land point as certain where the byelaws are in doubt; put the operator to proof. Some airports (e.g. Newcastle) are debated.
- **Grace periods don't help in drop-off zones.** Under the Single Code (Annex B), short-stay areas, including airport drop-off zones, get no grace period, and a minimum consideration period of only 1 minute. Don't claim the general "5 minutes" for a drop-off zone. But 1 minute is a minimum too (C6): if queues, a confusing layout or the time to find and read the payment terms meant the driver needed longer, argue that.
- ***Jopson* (stopping isn't parking) is weak for paid drop-off zones**, because the breach is the unpaid fee, not parking.
- **If the driver has been identified,** AP1 falls away; use the contract and signage points (C1, CP1–CP3) and get forum help.

### AP1. Airport byelaw land: no keeper liability (keeper appeal)
- **Point:** this is L3 and L4 applied to airports. The drop-off zone is airport land under byelaws, so it isn't "relevant land" and POFA keeper liability can never apply. The keeper isn't the driver in law, the driver hasn't been named, and liability can't pass from driver to keeper. So the operator has no one it can hold liable.
- **Applies when:** the location is inside the airport's byelaw boundary; the driver hasn't been identified; the appeal is from the keeper.
- **Evidence:** the incident location and date; the airport's byelaws and boundary map (6654021).
- **Strength:** Strong against APCOA and NCP, which usually cancel (6500170; success reports 6497331 Birmingham, 6503117 Heathrow). Against VCS and UKPPO (IPC) it's usually rejected at appeal and IAS; it does its work if a claim is brought (Moderate–strong in court, depending on that airport's byelaws). *Confidence: Medium* (forum reports, not a court ruling; byelaws vary by airport).
- **Defeated by:** the driver being named; a location outside the byelaw boundary; byelaws that don't cover stopping or parking at that airport.
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal:* the template below.
  - *Complaint (appeal time expired):* the same wording, sent as a complaint.
  - *POPLA:* set out the byelaws, the boundary and L4; add AP2 if the NTK claims POFA liability.
  - *IAS:* as POPLA, with the IAS pre-emptive points.
  - *Defence:* plead L3 and L4 with the airport's byelaws.
- **If the byelaw boundary isn't confirmed** (e.g. a car park on the edge of the airport, or the boundary map isn't to hand): don't assert byelaw land as fact. Use put-to-proof wording instead: "This location appears to be airport land subject to byelaws and therefore not 'relevant land' under Schedule 4 of the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012. You are put to strict proof that it is relevant land; otherwise there is no keeper liability." Check the boundary (6654021) before POPLA.
- **Established wording:** *(keeper appeal; template credited to ChirpyChicken in 6500170)* "This is an appeal by the registered keeper - No driver details will be given. … Due to this being airport land you will be well aware there is no keeper liability due to the byelaws in force and the fact this is not relevant land. As there is no keeper liability then liability cannot flow from the driver to the keeper … Please therefore cancel the notice or issue a POPLA code." The full template, in a more combative tone, is in the thread's opening post. Point the user there, and adapt the tone to the user.
- **Forum threads:** 6500170; 6497331; 6503117; 6654021.

### AP2. The NTK claims POFA keeper liability on land where it can't apply
- **Point:** Single Code cl. 8.1.1(d) says a notice must not "state the keeper is liable under the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 where they cannot be held liable". An NTK for byelaw airport land that claims POFA keeper liability breaches the Code, and it misleads the keeper.
- **Applies when:** the NTK states or implies that POFA keeper liability can apply (e.g. "we may pursue the registered keeper under the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012"), for a location inside the byelaw boundary. Implying statutory authority that doesn't exist is also covered by cl. 8.1.1(a).
- **Evidence:** the NTK wording; the byelaws and boundary map.
- **Strength:** Moderate at POPLA and IAS (a Code breach); supporting in court. Also grounds for a complaint to the trade body (and the DVLA for misuse of keeper data).
- **By stage:**
  - *Appeal and POPLA/IAS:* one or two sentences quoting cl. 8.1.1(d) and the NTK's wording.
  - *Defence:* supporting only, alongside AP1.
- **Established wording:** *(none yet)*
- **Forum threads:** 6500170 (NSL).
- **Sources:** Single Code v1.1 cl. 8.1.1(d) (`notices-and-codes.md`).
